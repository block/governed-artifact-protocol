A blog website publishes articles for people to read. Its content management system (CMS) gives the team a place to write posts, review them, and choose which versions appear on the website.

In this example, the people who manage content for a public garden's website are preparing an article for readers in the US and Mexico, in English and Spanish for each country. The headline and most of the article are the same within each language. One sentence tells readers when and where to see monarchs: at the garden during autumn migration in the US, or at the sanctuaries during winter in Mexico. A writer drafts the post, an editor reviews it, and a publisher makes the approved version available to readers.

The CMS uses [Governed Artifact Protocol (GAP)](/intro/what-is-gap) to connect the writer's saved draft to the editor's review and the publisher's authorization. When the website retrieves the published post, it receives those records too. It can then check that the content it displays is the version the editor approved and the publisher authorized.

Saving, reviewing, and publishing remain separate [actions](/intro/application-actions) in this CMS. Each carries out the GAP [lifecycle steps](/intro/lifecycle) or records the release decisions shown below:

## The blog post and its content model

The CMS stores the monarch butterfly post as a GAP [artifact](/specification/working-draft#artifact) with the ID `blog-post:monarch-migration`. Saving a change creates a new version of the post under that same ID, and earlier versions remain available. A [profile](/specification/working-draft#profile) defines the post's content model, including its fields and the rules they must satisfy. The blog post profile has four fields:

- `author` is the byline: the name shown on the post.
- `date` is the byline date and time, in RFC 3339 form, such as `2026-10-26T09:30:00Z`. The writer sets it as part of the content; the moment of publication is recorded on the authorization.
- `audience` is `public`, `members`, or `internal`, and the CMS uses it to choose which released posts each reader gets.
- `content` holds a headline and body for each locale: a language and country identified by a BCP 47 tag. The model requires US English and Mexican Spanish. US Spanish and Mexican English are optional; a missing entry falls back to the required language for the same country, preserving that country’s advice.

| Locale | Required content | Fallback if absent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| US English (`en-US`) | Yes | None; the save fails |
| US Spanish (`es-US`) | No | US English (`en-US`) |
| Mexican English (`en-MX`) | No | Mexican Spanish (`es-MX`) |
| Mexican Spanish (`es-MX`) | Yes | None; the save fails |

The CMS stores all authored [locale variants](/specification/working-draft#locale-variants) in one payload. Approval and publication apply to that whole version, including every locale it contains.

<Record set="cms" name="profile-revision" title="The blog-post profile, revision 1" collapsed />

Editing a headline creates a new version of the post using the same content model. Adding a required summary field changes the model itself, so the content model owner must ratify a new profile revision. Earlier post versions still reference the revision they were created under. Each post record's `profile` block includes the profile ID, revision number, and digest, so a reader can identify the exact rules used to check that version.

## From draft to published post

The monarch post moves from a writer's draft to the published page through these actions. Open the records under each action to see what the CMS stores.

### Set up the content model

<Steps block ids="propose-profile ratify-profile" />

A content designer proposes the post's fields, and the content model owner ratifies that exact [profile revision](/specification/working-draft#profile-revision), with a reason: "The model supports English and Spanish for readers in the US and Mexico, requiring US English and Mexican Spanish, with fallback within each country." Writers can use this ratified revision for any number of posts. Changing the content model requires a new revision.

### Save a draft

<Steps block ids="create-version" />

A writer saves the post. The CMS checks it against the profile revision and stores it as a new [artifact version](/specification/working-draft#artifact-version), keeping every earlier one. Until it is released, the post is a [draft](/specification/working-draft#draft) that only people and AI agents inside the CMS can see. When an editor asks for changes, the writer saves again, creating another version.

### Approve or reject

<Steps block ids="approve-release reject-release" />

The reviewer, an AI agent, reads version 1 and chooses Reject. The CMS records a [release rejection](/specification/working-draft#release-rejection) for that exact version. This CMS requires a reason on every rejection, so the writer knows what to change: "The sentence about where to see monarchs gives different places and seasons for the US and Mexico. Please use the same advice in all four versions."

This CMS allows an editor to approve a version after a reviewer has rejected it. The editor, a person, reads version 1, considers the reviewer's objection, and chooses Approve. The CMS records a [release approval](/specification/working-draft#release-approval) tied to that exact version, down to its payload digest and profile revision, with the editor's reason: "The viewing advice should differ: readers in the US can visit the garden during autumn migration, while readers in Mexico can visit the sanctuaries during winter. The headline and the rest of the article match within each language. Approving all four versions as written." The post is still a draft, and the approval covers version 1 only. Once a version is released, the CMS accepts no new approvals of it, because an approval is a decision made before publication.

The seasonal advice reflects [autumn migration in the US](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/monarchs-in-cuyahoga-valley.htm) and [winter sanctuary visits in Mexico](https://www.gob.mx/semarnat/acciones-y-programas/acerca-de-los-santuarios-de-la-mariposa-monarca).

The writer could instead revise the post and save a new version for review. The rejection would remain on the record of the earlier version.

### Publish

<Steps block ids="authorize-release release" />

A publisher reviews the approved version and chooses Publish. In one operation, the CMS records a [release authorization](/specification/working-draft#release-authorization) and the [release](/specification/working-draft#release), and the post becomes available to readers. This CMS publishes a version only after it has been approved; GAP leaves it to each application whether to require that.

The publisher gives a reason too: "The editor has reviewed the regional advice for both countries. Publish all four versions together." The post's [release proof](/specification/working-draft#release-proof) now carries the reviewer's rejection, the editor's approval, and the publisher's authorization, each with its reason and each covering version 1 only.

### Read the post

<Steps block ids="verify" />

The website retrieves the released post and its release proof, checks the proof, and renders the content using its own templates. It shows the requested language when available, or uses the fallback defined in the profile. A reader who wants to inspect the publication decisions can check the same proof.

## Why each decision was made

The content model owner, reviewer, editor, and publisher each give a [reason](/specification/working-draft#reason) for their decision. The release proof includes those decisions and reasons. Someone inspecting it later can read the reviewer's objection, the editor's response, and the publisher's explanation alongside the recorded names and times.

The record attributes the reason to the person or AI agent who made the decision. The decision identifies the same version whether or not a reason is supplied; the reason is excluded when GAP calculates the digests. GAP makes reasons optional; when supplied, they must contain text. This CMS also limits reasons to 5,000 characters and requires one on every rejection.

## Who does what?

The CMS decides who may draft, approve, and publish, and GAP lets a person or an AI agent hold any of those permissions. In this example, the writer and the reviewer are AI agents, and the editor and the publisher are people. GAP's [roles](/specification/working-draft#protocol-roles) treat them all the same.

The writer acts as [Author](/specification/working-draft#author). The reviewer and the editor act as [Authority](/specification/working-draft#authority) when they reject or approve a version, and the publisher acts as Authority when publishing it. The CMS grants each of those permissions separately, so being able to approve doesn't mean being able to publish. For the content model, the content designer is the Author and the content model owner is the Authority. An editor who revises a post is acting as Author.

The CMS is the [Implementation](/specification/working-draft#implementation): it checks content, keeps the records, and controls release. The website is a [Consumer](/specification/working-draft#consumer) of released content.

## Driving the specification

The blog demo implements the [working draft](/specification/working-draft) independently of the other demos. Building the editorial workflow helps identify rules that are missing or unclear. Those findings lead to draft changes or are recorded as [open questions](https://github.com/block/governed-artifact-protocol/blob/main/DEFERRED.md). The example records are updated when the draft changes.
